"I'll be strict for a month, then eat normally again."

Look at the word normally. If normally means the way of eating that put the weight on, you get the same result. A diet with an end date runs out. What lasts is a change you'd still keep a year from now.

Yo-yo dieting usually comes from a bad setup. Your character has little to do with it. Three things show up together: a deficit that's too big, muscle lost along the way, and a plan that was always going to stop.

Less muscle lowers what you burn at rest a little. When the old portions come back, there's less room for them.

There's a second push, and it isn't in your head. Sumithran and colleagues (2011) put 50 people on a very low calorie diet. A year later, in those who finished, appetite hormones still hadn't returned to pre-diet levels. Hunger stays louder. Willpower doesn't do much against that.

A different study followed 14 people from an extreme weight-loss competition for six years. Their resting metabolism was still about 700 kcal a day below where it started, roughly 500 kcal more than their new body composition and age would explain. That was a TV competition with extreme training and dieting. It says little about your mild deficit. There, the picture I see is different: you want the old portions, and the hunger lasts longer than you hoped. The harder you cut, the harder the body defends the old weight.

The setup I see hold up best:

- a mild deficit, 15 to 20% below your energy use
- protein around 1.6 to 2 g per kg
- lifting, so the body has a reason to keep muscle
- after the diet, add calories back to maintenance step by step, and stay there for a while

What keeps the weight off is a way of eating you can live with, long after the month is over.
