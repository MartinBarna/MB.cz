A bag of salty sticks in the hotel room is gone in ten minutes. A plate of potatoes and chicken isn't. There's a measured reason for that.

Kevin Hall kept 20 adults on a ward for a month (2019). Two weeks on ultra-processed food, two weeks on unprocessed food. Both menus offered matched calories, macros, sugar, salt and fiber, and people could eat as much as they wanted. On the ultra-processed menu they ate 508 kcal a day more and gained 0.9 kg in those two weeks. On the unprocessed menu they lost 0.9 kg.

The physics worked as usual. People gained because they ate more, and the food let them eat more without noticing.

The lever is speed. A co-author, Ciaran Forde, looked at how fast people ate in that trial. Ultra-processed: 48 kcal a minute. Unprocessed: 31. The fullness signal arrives tens of minutes late. Eat the same energy in half the time and "I've had enough" shows up when you're already a few hundred calories ahead.

Texture matters more than the label. In another trial, harder versions of the same lunches were eaten more slowly, and people took in 26% less energy from them.

What you can do with any menu or minibar, without banning anything:

- Pick the harder, bulkier version of the same food. Potatoes instead of mash. Nuts instead of a bar.
- Put the soft, dense food on a plate. Don't eat it from the bag.
