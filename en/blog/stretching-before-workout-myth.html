Stretching before a workout does not prevent injury

📅 22 October 2026 · ⏱️ 7 min read

Author: Martin Barna · Published 22 October 2026

"Do not forget to stretch properly before you train, so you do not get hurt." Almost everyone has heard that, from a school PE teacher to a video online. It sounds reasonable: you lengthen the muscles, you loosen up, and the body is ready. Look at what we actually know, and it comes out differently. A long static stretch before strength training does not protect you from injury. It can also take a little strength off you for a short while. It is not a duty. And it is not the insurance policy most people still think it is.

Short answer: No. A long static stretch before strength training does not protect you from injury, and it can briefly take a little strength off you. Before you train, 5-10 minutes of a dynamic warm-up, getting warm, and ramp-up sets make more sense. Gym injuries are driven mainly by sensible load progression, technique, and recovery.

What "static stretching" actually means

Clear the terms first, because half the confusion lives here. Static stretching is the classic version: you get into a position where you feel a pull in the muscle, and you hold it for a long time. A fold toward a straight leg, a wide straddle, pulling an arm across the body. The muscles are basically still, and you take them to an end position.

That version was sold for decades as injury insurance before every effort. The logic was that a "loose, lengthened muscle is harder to tear." It sounds nice. The reality is duller, and a lot more useful once you know it.

Static stretching before training does not lower injury risk

This is the core of the article. Put together what we know about stretching before strength and performance training, and one thing is clear: a long static stretch right before the effort does not meaningfully reduce injury. People who static-stretch before they train do not get injured less than people who skip it. The protection the whole ritual is built on is simply not there.

It goes one step further. If you take muscles to an end position and hold them there for a long time, right before strength or explosive work, you can mildly cut strength, power, and performance for a few dozen minutes. Nothing dramatic. The body comes back from it. But it is the opposite of what most people expect from the ritual: instead of switching them on, it dulls them a little.

Static stretching before you train is not a duty and it is not insurance. It does not lower injury risk, and it is more likely to take a little strength off you for a short while than to add any.

What actually gets you ready to train

This is not an argument for rolling off a chair and loading the bar to your max. Getting ready matters. It is just a different job than static stretching. What actually switches the body on:

  • A dynamic warm-up. Full-range movement, but moving: arm circles, lunges, squats with no load, arm swings, easy hip and spine motion. That wakes up the joints, the muscles, and the nervous system better than holding a still end position.
  • Getting warm. A few minutes that raise your temperature and leave you a little out of breath: a brisk walk, a bike, a few easy minutes. A warmer muscle is more compliant and works better.
  • Ramp-up sets of the exercise you are about to do. This is the most underrated part, and the most important. Before the working weight, you take the movement with an empty bar, then with gradually heavier loads. You groove the technique, the range of motion, and your focus for what is coming.

That trio prepares the joints, the muscles, and the nervous system better than any length of static stretching. And it does not eat the session: a few minutes, and you are into the work.

What actually drives gym injuries

This is why the worry about stretching points the wrong way. Whether you get hurt in the gym is not decided by whether you spent ten minutes pulling on your hamstrings first. Three other things decide it:

  • Sensible load progression. The great majority of gym injuries come from adding too much, too fast: weight, volume, or both. The body does not have time to adapt. Adding load patiently, in small steps, protects you far more than any stretch.
  • Technique. An exercise done with reasonable technique, through a controlled range, loads the body the way it is supposed to. Sloppy technique under a heavy bar is the problem, not the stretch you skipped.
  • Enough recovery. Sleep, food, days off. A tired, under-slept body makes mistakes and holds technique worse. Fatigue is a risk people forget.

Static stretching has a place. It is just somewhere else

This should not land as "stretching is bad." It is not. It just does not belong in front of the workout as an insurance policy. After training, or on its own at another time, static stretching has a perfectly legitimate job: when you want to improve or keep mobility and range of motion. If some range is limited, if a muscle is tight, and you want more room at the hip or the shoulder, static stretching is a good tool for that.

The difference is the reason. Stretch because you want better mobility, not because you are afraid you will tear something in a squat if you do not. The first reason makes sense. The second is needless fear.

"You have to stretch or you will get hurt" is needless fear

A lot of people carry that sentence as an obligation, and some guilt when they skip it. It is unnecessary. In ordinary strength training, nothing bad happens because you did not hold a long static stretch first. A decent warm-up, getting warm, and ramp-up sets are enough. Then you train.

If you treat pre-workout stretching as required insurance, you only load yourself with the feeling that you are doing something wrong. The real insurance (sensible progression, clean technique, and recovery) sits somewhere else. Put your energy there.

What to do with this

No ritual built on fear. Set it up like this:

  • Start with 5-10 minutes of a dynamic warm-up. Move, warm up a little, and take the patterns through a full range.
  • Then do ramp-up sets of the exercise. From the empty bar up toward the working weight. This is your best preparation for the effort and for your technique.
  • Do static stretching at another time, after training or on another day, when you want more range and mobility. Not as insurance before the work.
  • Watch progression and technique first. Add load reasonably, lift cleanly, and get your sleep. That is what decides whether you stay in one piece.

So the next time someone tells you that you "have to stretch properly or you will get hurt," you already know how that holds up. Static stretching before the effort does not protect you from injury, and it is more likely to take a little strength off you for a short while. What gets you ready is a dynamic warm-up and ramp-up sets. What actually protects you is sensible progression, technique, and recovery. Stretch when you want better mobility, not out of fear. Frequently asked questions

Why can stretching before a workout reduce strength?

If you take muscles to an end position and hold them there for a long time, right before strength or explosive work, you can mildly cut strength, power, and performance for a few dozen minutes. Nothing dramatic. The body comes back from it.

How should I warm up before strength training?

Start with 5-10 minutes of a dynamic warm-up, meaning full-range movement while you are moving, and add something that raises your temperature. Then do ramp-up sets of the exercise, from an empty bar up toward the working weight.

What decides whether you get injured in the gym?

Sensible load progression, technique, and enough recovery. The great majority of gym injuries come from adding too much, too fast: weight, volume, or both.

When does static stretching make sense?
