Does fat make you fat? The myth of fat and "light" foods

📅 19 October 2026 · ⏱️ 7 min read

Author: Martin Barna · Published 19 October 2026

"Fat in your food gets stored as fat on your body." "Want to lose weight? Throw out the butter, the oils, the nuts, and buy everything light." It sounds logical. Fat and fat, same word, so it must be connected. It is not connected the way the myth says. You gain weight when you take in more calories than you burn, not because there was fat on your plate.

Short answer: You gain from extra calories, wherever they come from. Fat is just more energy dense, about 9 kcal (calories) per gram against about 4 for protein and carbohydrate, so it is easier to overeat. A "light" label by itself will not make you lose a single gram.

Where the myth came from

The myth has a clear origin. In the 1980s and 1990s came the big "low-fat" wave. Fat was declared the main cause of weight gain, and the shelves filled up with low-fat versions of everything. The logic looked simple: fat has the most calories per gram, so cut it and you lose weight.

When you take fat out of food, you have to replace it with something so it still tastes like anything. That something was mostly sugar and starch. The result: food was often less filling, people got hungry sooner, ate more, and many did not lose weight. That era left behind the idea "fat = weight gain", and it has survived to this day, even though the simple story does not hold up: fat loss follows your calorie balance, whichever macro the calories come from.

Fat on your plate vs fat on your body

This is the core of the whole mistake. Same word, two different things. Eating fat does not automatically turn into rolls on your belly. What decides it is your total calorie balance over a longer stretch: whether you take in more than you burn.

What confuses people about fat is its energy density. Fat has about 9 kcal per gram, protein and carbohydrate about 4. That simply makes fat denser: more calories fit into a small portion. A tablespoon of oil (about 13-14 g) has around 120 kcal and you do not even notice you ate something. That is the whole catch. Fat does not make you fat by itself, but it is easy to pile up a lot of calories with it when you do not watch portions.

Why "light" often does not help (and what goes into it)

"Light", "low-fat" or "fat-free" on the pack sounds like a promise of weight loss. Often it is a trap. When a manufacturer takes the fat out, it has to put the taste and texture back. So it adds sugar, starch, thickeners or various substitutes. You grab a light yogurt feeling like you made a good choice, and it can have more sugar than the full-fat version.

Two things happen at the same time. The food tends to be less satisfying. Fat slows digestion a little and makes food taste good, which helps you feel satisfied. Most of the fullness, though, comes from protein, fibre and volume. And the calorie saving is often smaller than you would expect, and sometimes close to zero. So you may get hungry sooner and save fewer calories than you hoped. "Light" on the pack only tells you there is less fat. It says little about total calories or how healthy the food is.

Full-fat plain yogurt vs light sweetened yogurt:

  • Fat: higher in the full-fat one (from the milk), low to zero in the light one.
  • Sugar: the full-fat one has only the natural sugar from the milk. The light one often has added sugar, so more of it.
  • Calories: often closer than you would expect. Check the labels, the light one is not automatically lower.
  • Fullness: the plain one without added sugar tends to keep you satisfied longer. The sweetened one is easy to eat and hunger can come back sooner.

Here the sweetened light one is no win. The plain full-fat version has no added sugar, and the calorie gap is often smaller than you would expect. The fair comparison, though, is plain vs plain. A plain low-fat yogurt, skyr or quark is lower in calories and a great protein source in a deficit. The trap is the sweetened light versions. That does not mean you should write off light products. Read them critically, by the numbers, not by the words on the front.

Your body needs fat

Fat is not just "extra calories" you put up with. It is an essential nutrient. Your body gets essential fatty acids from it, which it cannot make by itself. Without fat in your food, you absorb vitamins A, D, E and K worse, because they dissolve in fat. Fat also plays a role in making hormones.

Then there is the practical side: fat makes food taste good and helps meals feel satisfying. Cut it hard from your diet and all you build is a "dry" diet you will not keep, and if you keep fat very low for a long time, it gets harder to cover essential fatty acids and fat-soluble vitamins. Cutting fat as far as possible usually backfires.

Fullness: why a sensible amount of fat helps you stick to a diet

This is what the low-fat camp missed. The calorie numbers decide fat loss. Whether you hit them depends on how long you can keep going. A sensible share of fat makes that easier. Good sources like olive oil, nuts, fish, eggs or avocado make meals tasty and satisfying, and food you enjoy is food you keep eating. When the food is good and you are not hungry an hour later, you hold the deficit much better.

A "dry" low-fat diet, where you are scared of every drop of oil, tends to be boring and hunger comes fast. And a diet you hate and that leaves you hungry is a diet you will drop sooner or later. So a bit of fat in your food can make the deficit easier to hold. How the three macros split the work is in macros and fat loss.

What to do in practice

In practice, watch four things:

  • Total calories and protein. This is the main thing. Fit into your calories and get enough protein, and for fat loss itself, it matters much less whether a meal was higher in fat or not. Your numbers are in the calculator.
  • Portions of energy-dense foods. Oils, nuts, butter, nut butters: this is where calories slip away fast. You do not have to throw them out, just measure them and count them. A handful of nuts (about 30 g) is roughly 180-200 kcal, and it rarely stays at one handful.
  • The quality of your fat sources. Olive oil, fish, nuts, eggs, avocado: these belong in your diet. Pick good fat sources and measure the dense ones.
  • Read "light" critically. Turn the pack over and look at the calories and the sugar, not the words on the front. If the light version has the same or more calories and more sugar, it is no win.

So next time you hear "fat makes you fat", or you reach for something only because it says "light", stop for a second. What matters is fitting into your calories and getting enough protein. Fat belongs in a normal diet. And the "light" label? It promises nothing. The back of the pack tells you what you need to know. Be Effective!
