Intermittent fasting: what the studies say

📅 13 October 2026 · ⏱️ 4 min read

Author: Martin Barna · Published 13 October 2026

Intermittent fasting has a reputation as a miracle. "Eat in an 8-hour window and you lose weight without counting calories." Sounds nice. In June 2025 the BMJ published the biggest analysis on this topic so far. It pooled 99 randomized trials and 6,582 people. The result is sober: fasting works, and there is no magic in it.

Short answer: Intermittent fasting does not work better than classic calorie counting. The analysis of 99 randomized trials and 6,582 people, published in the BMJ in June 2025, found small to no differences between fasting and a classic deficit. Over the long run (6+ months) all approaches worked about the same, and the winner was the one a person could stick to.

What the big analysis found

The researchers compared three common forms of fasting with a classic calorie deficit (simply eating less every day) and with eating without limits. The three forms were eating in a time window (for example 16:8), alternating days with food and without, and whole-day fasts.

  • Fasting and a classic deficit both work. Both lead to weight loss compared with eating without limits.
  • Fasting is no better than counting calories. The differences between intermittent fasting and a classic deficit were small to none.
  • One form, alternating a "hungry" day with a normal day, did slightly better (about 1.3 kg extra). That was mostly in short studies up to half a year. In longer ones the difference disappeared.
  • Over the long run (6+ months) all approaches worked about the same. The winner was the one a person could stick to.

Why fasting works at all

Fasting has no secret fat-burning route. It works for one simple reason: when you shrink the window in which you eat, many people simply eat less over the day. You skip breakfast, you do not snack in the evening in front of the TV. You are in a deficit without counting it. The mechanism is energy balance maths. Fasting is one of many ways to get into a deficit.

That is good news, because you can choose what suits you. Some people do well eating twice a day, others five times. Neither is more "correct". What decides is what you can stick to and how much you eat in total.

Who it fits, and who it does not

Try it if you do not like breakfast, you snack mostly in the evening, or you like a clear rule: "now we do not eat". A narrower window may lower your intake naturally.

Do not force it if hunger ruins your focus and mood, you train early in the morning, or you tend to overeat in the window so that the deficit disappears anyway. And watch out: if you stack pizza, cola and dessert into the eating window, no fasting will save you. What and how much you eat still matters most.

Fasting is not for everyone. If you are pregnant, have an eating disorder, or take medication for diabetes, talk to your doctor first.

What to take from it

Intermittent fasting is a legitimate tool, no better and no worse than a classic deficit. If it helps you eat less and keep it up, great. If it only stresses you out, drop it and work on your total intake another way. The time of day you eat matters little. What counts is how much you eat in a day, and whether you stick with it long enough for it to show.
