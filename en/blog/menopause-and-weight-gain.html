Menopause and weight gain: what actually works

📅 13 October 2026 · ⏱️ 6 min read

Author: Martin Barna · Published 13 October 2026

I hear the same story from a lot of women. For years you ate the same and moved the same, and suddenly the scale climbs, and the fat sits differently, around the middle. Then the line from other people, and from you: "You have to try harder." Stop there. The menopause years really do change your body at the hormone level. It is not about willpower.

Short answer: Estrogen falls, fat shifts toward the belly, energy expenditure drops and muscle declines, so the same food is suddenly too much. Your body changed, your effort did not. What helps most is strength training, plus 1.2 to 1.6 g of protein per kg a day, and a modest calorie deficit.

What changes

In perimenopause and menopause, estrogen falls. Estrogen also steers your metabolism: where fat is stored, how the body burns it, and how insulin behaves.

When estrogen drops, several things happen at once. Fat shifts from the hips and thighs toward the belly. That includes visceral fat, the fat around the organs, which raises cardiometabolic risk. A narrative review of women whose estrogen falls early (premature ovarian insufficiency) describes central fat storage, higher lipolysis in visceral fat and worse insulin sensitivity. Similar mechanisms are likely at work in natural menopause.

In short: the same meals as at 35 now behave differently. If the weight gain is sudden or comes with other changes, have it checked by your doctor. The wider picture of fat loss in these years is in fat loss after 50.

Why your body needs less now

Three things change at the same time.

  • A lower energy burn. A narrative review reports that through the menopause transition resting and sleeping energy expenditure tend to fall, and so does the body's use of fat as fuel. The body simply uses less, even when you do nothing.
  • Less muscle. Muscle burns a little more at rest than fat, and it keeps you strong. A review describes loss of muscle (sarcopenia) as common in this transition and links it to hormonal changes, including falling estrogen, testosterone and growth hormone.
  • More fat around the middle at the same scale weight. Even if the number does not move, the composition shifts toward more fat and less muscle.

Add it up: a smaller burn plus less muscle means the same food is suddenly extra.

Strength training comes first

If you take one thing from this piece, take this: start lifting. Weights, bands or your own body weight. Cardio is good for your heart, but it will not hold your muscle the way lifting does.

Strength training is the thing that fights the loss of muscle directly. A systematic review of studies in postmenopausal women that paired strength training with nutrition strategies found improvements in body composition and strength. Effects on bone density tend to be smaller and less consistent. Bone density is the second big topic in these years. It protects you from fractures. If you have osteoporosis, joint problems or a heart condition, check with your doctor or physio before you start.

A review of the training programs shows what was used most often in practice: compound lifts for the whole body (squats, leg press, chest press), typically 3 sets of 8 to 10 reps. You do not have to train like a bodybuilder. What matters is doing it regularly and adding load over time. The fear that weights will make you bulky is answered in will lifting make women bulky.

Protein on the plate

Without enough protein, even the best training will not hold your muscle. If you lift and you want to keep it, aim for about 1.2 to 1.6 g of protein per kg of body weight a day, and toward the top of that if you are losing fat. The classic 0.8 g per kg is a general minimum for sedentary people, and it is usually not enough to hold muscle while you train. A wider look at protein, outside this life stage, is in how much protein.

In practice that means protein in every meal: eggs or quark (a fresh cheese, similar to strained Greek yogurt) at breakfast, meat, fish or legumes at lunch and at dinner, Greek yogurt as a snack. The point is simply that protein shows up at all. In my practice, protein is the thing women at this stage most often fall short on.

With the protein: plenty of vegetables, a sensible amount of carbohydrate around training, and fats you tolerate. You do not need to cut out any food group. The plate should support your muscle and keep you full.

Deficit, sleep, and patience

If you want to lose fat, a calorie deficit still applies. In these years it pays to build it carefully. A systematic review reports that adding a modest calorie deficit to strength training led to more fat loss. Lifting plus a modest deficit plus enough protein is one of the better setups: you lose fat and you hold the muscle.

Do not shrug off sleep. Poor sleep raises appetite, worsens insulin sensitivity and undercuts recovery. Hot flushes and insomnia in these years can wreck sleep. Decent sleep is part of fat loss. The numbers on short sleep are in sleep and fat loss.

And patience: body composition changes over months, not days. No fat burner, no tea and no miracle supplement gets around this. Creatine has reasonable evidence for supporting strength training in women at this age. Vitamin D matters mainly if you are low, and your doctor can test that. Neither replaces lifting, protein and sleep.

What about hormone therapy?

Hormone therapy is a real medical topic. For some women it helps a lot with symptoms such as hot flushes and poor sleep. Its effect on weight is modest. Some studies suggest it may limit fat gain around the middle, but it does not replace training and food. It belongs with your doctor. It is an individual decision that depends on your health, your history and your risks. Talk to your doctor. Do not adjust medication on your own.

I do not prescribe it and I do not tell you whether to take it. What works whether you use it or not is the training, the food and the routine: strength, muscle, and health through these years. That is the base.
