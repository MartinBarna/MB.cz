Sore muscles are not a sign of a better workout

📅 22 October 2026 · ⏱️ 6 min read

Author: Martin Barna · Published 22 October 2026

The day after a workout your legs hurt so much you can barely get down the stairs. You tell yourself, "That one counted. I grew from that." When nothing hurts, you feel like you coasted, and that tomorrow you will look the same as yesterday. The idea that soreness equals an effective workout sounds logical, and plenty of people still carry it around. Next-day pain tells you almost nothing about muscle growth. Here is why.

Short answer: No. Sore muscles (DOMS) are mostly a response to something unfamiliar, like a new exercise or more sets than usual, and they tell you almost nothing about growth. Muscle grows from progressive overload, enough protein and energy, and recovery and sleep. You can grow very well with almost no soreness.

What soreness (DOMS) actually is

The technical name is DOMS: delayed onset muscle soreness. It is that dull, tender ache that does not show up the moment you rack the bar. It comes on later and usually peaks about a day or two after the session. The important part: soreness is mostly a response to an unfamiliar stimulus. It shows up when you do something your body is not used to. A new exercise, a lot more sets than usual, a lot of lowering (eccentric) work, a longer range of motion.

It is not a sign that you "broke the muscle and now it will grow." It is closer to a signal that says "this was new." New and effective are not the same thing. An unfamiliar session can leave you very sore even when, for growth, it was no better than a workout that left you feeling fine.

What muscle actually grows from

The boring answer is the true one. Muscle grows from three things, and pain is not one of them:

  • Progressive overload. You gradually do more: more weight, more reps, more quality work over time. This is the main driver.
  • Enough protein, and enough energy overall. Without building material, muscle has nothing to grow from.
  • Recovery and sleep. Muscle does not grow in the gym. It grows between sessions, while you recover.

Notice that "how much it hurts the next day" is not on that list. That is the whole point. You can grow very well with almost no soreness, which is typical once you have a settled, well-built program your body understands. Feeling no pain does not mean the work did nothing. Often it means you are training sensibly.

"If it does not hurt, it did not work" is a trap

That line sounds motivating. In practice it costs you. Once you decide a workout only counts if you hurt the next day, you start chasing pain instead of progress. That leads exactly where you do not want to go:

  • Wrecking yourself for no reason. Instead of adding weight or reps in a straight line, you keep swapping exercises and piling on volume just so it hurts again. Your body never gets time to lock in progress where the work was already paying off.
  • Worse recovery. If you are always beaten up, you recover worse, you sleep worse, and the next session is weaker. A weaker session means less real work for growth.
  • A higher chance of injury and of doing too much. Chasing pain means overshooting the dose. That is a direct path to a strain, an irritated tendon, or a long stretch of fatigue that takes you out of training altogether.
Pain after training is not a reward for a job well done. It only tells you the stimulus was unfamiliar. The goal is growth, not soreness.

A lot of soreness is not more growth

This is the cleanest way to retire the myth. Big soreness does not mean big growth. Most of the time it only means you did something very unfamiliar: you came back after a layoff, you tried a brand-new exercise, you added a pile of lowering work.

There is a neat proof of how little soreness says about growth: the body gets used to the stimulus. Do the same workout a week later and it hurts less. The week after that, it barely hurts at all. The name for that is the repeated bout effect. The body adapts, and the same session no longer produces the same pain. You keep growing anyway. The ache faded. The growth did not. If soreness were the measuring stick, we would have to say that the longer you train, the less you grow. That is nonsense. It is the other way around.

What to track instead of pain

If you stop grading a workout by how sore you are, what do you use? These are the markers that actually tell you whether you are progressing:

What actually drives muscle growthWhat people track by mistake
Progress in load and reps over timeHow much they hurt the next day
Solid technique and a full range of motionWhether they "feel the burn" at all costs
A sensible number of sets across the weekHow many sets they took all the way to failure
Enough protein and enough energyFatigue and feeling wrecked, treated as proof
Sleep and recovery between sessionsThe feeling that the workout "was not hard enough"

If the weight and the reps in your log (or your app) are creeping up with clean technique, and you feel rested and strong, you are growing. Whether you are sore or not. Those are the markers. Pain is side noise.

What to do with this

No drama. A few simple rules:

  • Do not steer by soreness. Plan the progression: add a little weight or a rep over time. That is your guide, not pain.
  • Eat enough protein, and enough food overall. Muscle needs material and energy. Soreness does not build anything by itself.
  • Sleep, and recover. Sleep is the cheapest and most effective "supplement" there is for growth and for performance.
  • Treat pain as a side note, not a target. At most it tells you the stimulus was new. Do not read more into it.
  • If soreness shows up, ease it with light movement. A walk, some easy motion, a little activity. Blood flow helps it leave faster than a day on the couch.

So the next time nothing hurts the day after, and you start wondering if the session mattered, stop. Check whether the weight on the bar and the reps in your log are going up. That is your evidence, not the ache. And when something does leave you sore, read it as "that was new," not as a medal. Build on progress, protein, and sleep. Leave the pain alone. Be Effective!

Frequently asked questions

What is DOMS?

Delayed onset muscle soreness. A dull, tender pain that does not arrive right after training. It comes on later and usually peaks about a day or two afterward.

Why does the same workout barely hurt after a few weeks?

Your body gets used to the stimulus. The name for that is the repeated bout effect. The same workout no longer hurts as much, and you keep growing anyway.

What should I track instead of soreness?

Progress in load and reps over time, solid technique and a full range of motion, a sensible number of sets across the week, enough protein and energy, and sleep. If the weight and the reps are going up with clean technique and you feel rested and strong, you are growing, sore or not.

Why is chasing soreness a bad idea?

It pushes you to wreck yourself for no reason, recover worse, and perform worse next time. Chasing pain means overshooting the dose, which raises the chance of injury and of doing too much.

How do I ease soreness?

With light movement. A walk, some easy motion, or other light activity gets blood through the muscle, and the pain eases faster than it does if you stay on the couch.
