How much coffee a day, and what it actually does for you

📅 22 October 2026 · ⏱️ 7 min read

Author: Martin Barna · Published 22 October 2026

Almost every client asks me about coffee sooner or later. Usually in one of two ways: "is it bad for me?" and "will I burn more after it?" There is a decent amount of data on both.

Short answer: How much coffee a day is reasonable?
The lowest risks show up around 3 to 4 cups a day, compared with none. That is observational data and an average from large groups, not a prescription for you. If you drink coffee and you feel fine on it, the data does not give you a reason to quit. If you do not drink it, these studies are not a reason to start. In pregnancy, or if you are a woman at higher risk of fractures, this is a question for your doctor.

The numbers look best at 3 to 4 cups a day

One of the broadest reviews on this came out in The BMJ (Poole and colleagues, 2017, PMID 29167102). The authors did not walk through single studies. They pooled 201 meta-analyses of observational research, covering 67 different health outcomes. Across that pile, coffee drinking was linked with benefit more often than with harm.

The lowest risks landed, again and again, around 3 to 4 cups a day compared with drinking none:

  • all-cause mortality, relative risk (RR) 0.83 (confidence interval 0.79 to 0.88),
  • cardiovascular mortality 0.81 (0.72 to 0.90),
  • cardiovascular disease 0.85 (0.80 to 0.90).

A second paper goes into the heart in more detail. A meta-analysis in Circulation (Ding and colleagues, 2014, PMID 24201300) tracked how risk changes with the number of cups. It added up 36 studies, 1,279,804 people, and 36,352 cardiovascular events. The relationship was non-linear: risk was lowest in the middle range and drifted back towards the no-coffee level at the highest intake. Against people who drink no coffee, the lowest risk sat around 3.5 cups a day (RR 0.85). At 1.5 cups it was 0.89.

In the highest category, a median of 5 cups, the RR was 0.95, with an interval of 0.87 to 1.03. The interval crosses 1, so there is no proven effect there. The authors conclude that heavy intake was not associated with higher risk, which is not the same thing as saying it protected people. That part usually disappears from coffee articles. It is a shame, because it stops overclaiming in both directions.

A link is not a cause

The numbers I quoted from both papers come from observational data. Nobody assigned people their coffee. Researchers watched who drank how much, and how they did later. Poole says it plainly in the conclusion: to answer whether the relationship is causal, you need proper randomized trials.

Why I insist on that. A person who drinks four coffees at work in the morning differs, on average, from a person who drinks none, in a lot of other ways: job, sleep, smoking, age, health. Poole notes that the harmful associations of coffee mostly dissolved once the data was properly adjusted for smoking, with pregnancy as the exception. "Linked with" and "causes" are not the same sentence.

So the takeaway is calm, and it matches the short answer at the top.

Coffee and fat loss: there may be an effect, and it is small

This is where people are most disappointed, so here are the numbers. A meta-analysis of 13 randomized trials with 606 participants (Tabrizi and colleagues, 2019, PMID 30335479) found that doubling caffeine intake increased the average weight loss by 22%, the drop in BMI by 17%, and the loss of fat mass by 28%.

That sounds good until you look at what it means. In the trials, every doubling of the caffeine dose went with a 22% larger weight reduction. To put that in scale (my illustration, not a number from the study): if you are losing half a kilo a week (about 1 lb), 22% more is roughly 100 grams a week (about 4 oz). The studies also disagreed with each other enormously (the I-squared statistic, which measures that inconsistency, came out at 91 to 94%, and anything over 75% is a lot). The authors themselves land on a cautious conclusion: caffeine intake "might promote" weight, BMI and body fat reduction.

My position does not change because of that. The result is decided by energy in versus energy out. That is where fat loss actually happens, and coffee steps into it in tenths. What I see with clients is a different kind of help: coffee keeps them alert and makes training easier to start, so it shows up indirectly in how much they get done in a day. That is a more honest way to count it than treating it as a fat burner.

When to drink it so it does not wreck your sleep

For me this is the most important practical question about coffee, because sleep is one of the strongest levers you have while losing fat.

A placebo-controlled study (Drake and colleagues, 2013, PMID 24235903) gave people 400 mg of caffeine, roughly four cups of filter coffee at once, either right before bed, 3 hours before bed, or 6 hours before bed. Sleep was measured with a device, not only a questionnaire. All three timings disturbed sleep meaningfully compared with placebo. The authors take that to a simple recommendation: leave a larger dose of caffeine alone for at least six hours before bed.

In practice: if you go to bed at 11, 5 pm is the very latest for a bigger coffee, and earlier is better. In this study, even a dose taken six hours before bed still cut sleep. An espresso at 3 in the afternoon usually is fine. Four coffees at 6 in the evening are not. I wrote up how sleep ties to fat loss here.

What not to pour into it, and how to brew it

Most of the coffee problems I see with clients do not come from the coffee. They come from what is in it.

Calories. Black coffee is negligible. A large latte with syrup is already a snack, and it is usually missing from the food log. Three of those a day is hundreds of calories that nobody writes down, and on their own they can eat an entire deficit. You do not have to drop them. Log them and count them. This is the most common gap I find in a food log that involves coffee.

How you brew it. A controlled study of 121 healthy people (Strandhagen and Thelle, 2003, PMID 12947437) measured 600 ml of filter coffee a day, about four cups. Over four weeks, total cholesterol rose by 0.15 to 0.25 mmol/L. Three weeks with no coffee lowered it by 0.22 to 0.36 mmol/L. That was filter coffee. A review in the Ochsner Journal (Mendoza and colleagues, 2023, PMID 37323518) adds that boiled, unfiltered coffee is more atherogenic than filtered coffee because of diterpenes. It is a narrative review, a weaker kind of evidence than a meta-analysis, but it matches earlier findings summarised by Strandhagen: filtering removes about 80% of the cholesterol-raising effect of unfiltered coffee.

Practically: if you are working on cholesterol, filtered coffee is the better choice than a French press or Turkish coffee, and the amount matters too: four cups of filter coffee a day still nudged total cholesterol up. If you have high cholesterol or take medication for it, talk to your doctor. What moves LDL cholesterol much more than your choice of brewer is written up here.

Frequently asked questions

How much coffee a day is the sweet spot?

In the data, the band of 3 to 4 cups a day comes out best, but that is an average from watching large groups, not a prescription for you. Tolerance differs enough between people that your own sleep and a racing heart are a better guide than a chart. If coffee gives you palpitations, or you have a heart condition or high blood pressure, talk to your doctor about how much is right for you.

Will coffee speed up my metabolism enough to make me lose fat?

Not on its own. The trials suggest a possible effect on body weight, but it is small and the individual studies disagreed a lot. Fat loss is intake and expenditure. Coffee is an add-on.

Can I drink coffee in pregnancy?

Pregnancy, along with fractures in women, is one of two areas where the BMJ review found risks for coffee. That is a question for your doctor, not for a nutrition coach. The advice there differs from the general population, so the decision belongs with you and your doctor.
