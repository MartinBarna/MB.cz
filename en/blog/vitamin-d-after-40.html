Vitamin D after 40: what it is actually good for

📅 22 October 2026 · ⏱️ 9 min read

Author: Martin Barna · Published 22 October 2026

Vitamin D is probably the most overhyped supplement of the last ten years. To some people it is a miracle for everything. To others it is completely useless. The truth, as usual, sits in the middle, and it is fairly boring: for some people it makes sense, for some it does not, and more is definitely not better. Here is a practical, calm version for life after 40.

Short answer: Does vitamin D make sense after 40?
For some people yes, for some no, and more is definitely not better. Together with calcium it really does help bones, and in people with a very low level it can also help muscle function and stability. The data does not yet support it as a way to prevent colds. Do not guess. Have a doctor measure your blood level of 25-OH vitamin D.

What vitamin D is actually good for

Start with the good part. Vitamin D is not a placebo. Its main job is clear and well supported: it helps the body use calcium, and that supports bone health. After 40, and especially after menopause in women, bone mass naturally declines. Here vitamin D, together with calcium, plays a real role.

A 2025 review article sums it up as a pair: vitamin D on its own, without enough calcium coming in, does not do much for bone. A large share of people are short on both at the same time. So if you are looking at vitamin D for your bones, look at calcium in the diet at the same time (dairy, vegetables, and a supplement if you need one).

The second place where the data is decent: in people whose level is genuinely low, topping it up can help bones, and in some cases muscle function and stability too. It will not turn you into an athlete. The point is to get the body out of a deficit and into the normal range. The key word is deficit. More on that in a minute.

What it probably is not good for, even though people claim it

This is where I have to disappoint the marketing. Vitamin D is sold as an immunity booster, and as prevention for colds, flu, and respiratory infections. The reality is lukewarm.

A systematic review and meta-analysis from 2024 looked at whether vitamin D supplementation actually reduces acute respiratory infections in people with darker skin (a group at higher risk of deficiency). The result: no clear effect, and the authors explicitly say the evidence is still weak. In a similar way, a 2025 meta-analysis of supplementation in pregnancy did not show a meaningful benefit for respiratory and allergic problems in the children.

What to take from that: if your level is normal and you take vitamin D so you get sick less, you are probably buying peace of mind rather than a result. For immunity, sleep, enough protein, movement, and a sensible body weight usually do more than another capsule. Protein targets are in how much protein to eat.

And no, vitamin D is not a treatment for fatigue, depression, fat loss, or "metabolism." If someone sells it to you as a cure-all, step back. If you have been tired for a long time, or your mood has been flat, do not try to put that out with capsules on your own. That belongs with a doctor.

Who a deficiency actually applies to

First, decide whether you are even in a higher-risk group. Deficiency is not universal, and it is not rare either. At higher latitudes it shows up mainly from fall through spring, when the sun is not strong enough.

Your risk of a low level is higher if:

  • You hardly get any sun, or you block it completely (office work, winters spent indoors).
  • You have darker skin: you need more sun for the same effect.
  • You are older: skin makes vitamin D less well with age.
  • You have overweight or obesity: vitamin D "hides" in fat tissue.
  • You have osteoporosis or osteopenia, you are after a fracture, or you take corticosteroids long term.

Interesting data came from a 2025 study in older patients with several illnesses: about a third of those who, by the risk criteria, should have been taking vitamin D were not taking it, and on the other side about 10% were taking it even though they did not have a clear reason. Both are wrong. The goal is not "everyone takes it." It is "the person who has a reason takes it."

The surest way to find out: have a doctor measure your level (25-OH vitamin D) from a blood test. Do not guess. Measure it. And let a doctor tell you what to do with the result.

How much, and when: a sensible general dose

Straight up: your doctor should set the exact dose from your level and your health. So you have a picture of the guardrails, though.

A 2025 guideline built on a high-quality rating of the evidence (GRADE methods) recommends, for adults who need supplementation, a daily equivalent of about 600 to 2000 IU. And it explicitly prefers that range over routine doses above 2000 IU. That is a decent, safe frame for most people.

Practical points I repeat in the clinic:

  • Take it regularly, not in bursts. A small regular dose is more sensible than a megadose once in a while (why, in a moment).
  • Take it with a meal, ideally with a little fat. It absorbs better.
  • Think about it mainly in the colder half of the year. In summer, if you are outside, you often get by on the sun.
  • Do not forget calcium in the diet if you are doing this for your bones.

A 2025 review suggests that a sensible "band" for the blood level sits somewhere between 20 and 40 ng/mL: in that range, the risk of falls and fractures looks lowest. The goal is not to crank the level as high as it will go. It is to get into the normal range and stay there.

Why blanket megadoses do not make sense

This is the most important paragraph, so pay attention. There is an idea that if too little is bad, a lot must be great. For vitamin D, that is not true. The relationship looks like the letter U: risk rises at both ends.

A 2025 review says it plainly: both a low intake and an excessive intake of vitamin D are linked with a higher risk of falls and fractures. Yes, you read that right. Overdoing the dose is tied to risk, not to an extra bonus.

Why? A controlled study looked at single high bolus doses (up to 500,000 IU) in women who were deficient. A hit like that brought no improvement in physical function or strength, and in the short term it stirred up bone metabolism. The authors themselves add that the clinical meaning of that temporary change is uncertain. In other words: big hits did not help here, and they do not give you a safety guarantee.

So no "I will take a megadose and have peace for a month." That is exactly the path that does not make sense. A small regular dose, in a sensible range, is boring. That is precisely why it is the safer bet.

A practical summary after 40

If you take five sentences from this, take these:

  • Bones and calcium, and in a deficiency also muscle and stability: that is where vitamin D really helps.
  • Immunity and "fewer illnesses": the data does not support that yet. Do not take it mainly for that.
  • Do not guess. Have the level measured, and think about it mainly from fall through spring.
  • A sensible daily dose (generally around 600 to 2000 IU, as your doctor sets it) is a better choice than megadoses.
  • More is not better. The goal is to reach the normal range, not to overshoot it.

Vitamin D is a good servant for the job it has. It is not a miracle, and it is not a substitute for sleep, strength, protein, and movement. Do the basics. This is only a supplement.
