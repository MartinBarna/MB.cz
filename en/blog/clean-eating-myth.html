The clean eating myth: no food is dirty

📅 13 October 2026 · ⏱️ 6 min read

Author: Martin Barna · Published 13 October 2026

"Do not eat that, it is dirty food." "You have to eat clean or you will not lose fat." I hear it on repeat, and it bothers me, because it produces a lot of useless guilt and fear of food. The truth is dull, and it is freeing. No food is "clean" or "dirty" by itself. No food makes you fat by itself, and no food makes you healthy by itself. What you eat over weeks decides. One bite on its own changes very little.

Short answer: You do not have to eat clean to lose fat. No food is clean or dirty on its own, and no single meal makes you fat or healthy. The whole picture decides: calories, protein, fiber, and how long you can keep it. You lose fat from a deficit you can stay on.

"Clean" and "dirty" is a made-up label

Sorting foods into clean and dirty sounds like science. It is a moral label people invented. Biochemistry has no such category. Once absorbed, glucose is glucose, whether it came from homemade oatmeal or a bar. What differs is the food around it: fiber, fat and how fast it digests change how full you stay. Protein from chicken and from a decent protein dessert ends up as amino acids. Just check the label: some desserts use collagen or gelatin, which counts for less.

No single food makes you fat or healthy. How you look and how you feel comes from what you eat over time. An ice cream on a Tuesday is a small part of that. Call one food "dirty" and you are telling yourself that one meal can ruin an otherwise working diet. That is not true. How flexible this can be is in flexible dieting.

What actually moves the result

Drop the labels and a few things remain. None of them is the "cleanliness" of one food.

  • Are the day's calories in the right place? Gain and loss follow the total balance across days and weeks. See calorie deficit: how much to eat.
  • Did you get enough protein? It holds fullness, muscle and recovery. See how much protein.
  • Did you get enough fruit, vegetables and fiber? That is where fullness, digestion and micronutrients are decided. See fiber and fat loss.
  • Can you keep it? Consistency over weeks and months beats any single day.

There is no "banned food" on that list. Calories, protein, fiber and consistency. That is nutrition for a normal person who wants to lose fat and feel fine. A plate that does it is in what to eat to lose fat.

Clean eating often slides into anxiety

Split the food world into good and evil and anxiety grows easily: you keep checking whether something is clean enough, you feel guilty about a normal meal, you will not eat in a restaurant or at your parents'. Some people call an obsession with "healthy" food orthorexia. It is not an official diagnosis, but it can seriously affect your life. If food rules are running your day like that, talk to your doctor or an eating disorder specialist. I do not treat eating disorders. If you feel overwhelmed or unsafe, contact your local crisis line or emergency services (in the Czech Republic: 116 123).

There is a second catch. Forbidden fruit tastes the strongest. In my practice, strict bans often end the same way: a week of "clean", then overeating, then guilt, and the wheel turns again. A black-and-white relationship with food hurts you more than a piece of chocolate. Guilt about food rarely helps anyone stick to a plan.

80/20: a split you can keep

Instead of "all clean, or nothing", a simple rule works better in practice. Build most of the diet, say about eighty percent, on basic foods that fill you and carry nutrients. Meat, fish, eggs, dairy, pulses, grains, fruit, vegetables. Leave the rest for things you like: coffee with something sweet, a beer, chocolate, a favorite meal with family.

The body gets what it needs, the calories still fit, and you are not living in a cell. You can hold this for years. The best diet is the one you can still follow in six months.

Ultra-processed food is still easy to overeat, mostly because it does not keep you full for long. It is covered in ultra-processed food.

"Healthy" foods have calories too

The other side of the coin is just as slippery. A label like organic, fit, raw or superfood makes people feel they can eat as much as they want. The body counts calories, whatever the packaging says. Plenty of "healthy" foods are energy-dense:

  • Nuts and nut butters. A good food, and full of fat and calories. A handful is fine. Half a jar of peanut butter is roughly 700 to 1,000 kcal (calories), depending on the jar size.
  • Oils, including the best olive oil. A tablespoon of oil (about 120 kcal) has as much energy as several sugar cubes. "Healthy" fat is still a lot of energy.
  • Avocado. A useful food, and energy-dense. Half at breakfast is fine. Three a day add up to several hundred kcal.
  • Granola, muesli bars, "fit" desserts. They often have as much sugar and fat as a regular sweet, with a nicer wrapper and a higher price.

You can gain fat from "organic" granola just as easily as from the ordinary kind. And a sane amount of "unhealthy" chocolate will not make you gain. Same point again: amount and context decide.

What to do

  • Build the diet on the basics. Most of the food should be protein, fruit, vegetables, fiber, and carbohydrates and fats you can name. That is the spine.
  • Leave room for foods you like. On purpose, in a sane amount, without guilt. When you plan it, it is less likely to come back as a binge.
  • Watch calories and protein. That pair decides fat loss and, together with strength training, whether you keep muscle. Much more than the "cleanliness" of each meal.
  • Do not treat any food as banned. Treat it as a question of amount and context. "How much, and when", not "am I allowed".

Fat loss comes from a deficit you keep for months.
