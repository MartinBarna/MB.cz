Sarcopenia: how to keep your muscle and strength after 50

📅 22 October 2026 · ⏱️ 8 min read

Author: Martin Barna · Published 22 October 2026

Sarcopenia sounds like a diagnosis from a medical dictionary. It is simply the technical name for something that touches almost everyone: a gradual loss of muscle mass and strength with age. The good news? It is not fate. There are two levers that actually work, and both of them are fully in your hands. Let us take it apart without scare tactics and without miracles.

Short answer: How do you keep muscle and strength after 50?
With two levers: strength training 2 to 3 times a week, and enough protein. For older adults, many recommendations aim at about 1.2 to 1.5 g per kilogram of body weight a day. The two together work best. If you have diabetes, heart trouble, joint problems, or kidney disease, or you take medication, talk to your doctor before you change anything.

What sarcopenia is, and why it starts after 50

Muscle is not a fixed lump. Your whole life, the body builds it and breaks it down at the same time. Until about your forties, that stays in balance. Then the scale starts to tip, slowly, and you begin to lose muscle.

The numbers: a 2025 scientific review reports that after the fourth decade of life you lose about 8% of muscle mass per decade, and after 70 that speeds up to 15% per decade. That is not a small thing. And strength disappears even faster than the mass itself.

Why does it happen? Less movement, hormonal change, chronic low-grade inflammation, and the muscle handling protein less well. Add it up: the body stops keeping up with muscle it used to hold almost for free.

Why this is not about how you look

This is not about having nice arms on the beach. Muscle is your organ of independence. When it declines, the effects run through a whole life:

  • Falls and fractures. Weaker muscles mean worse stability. A review of sarcopenia and falls reports that falls are the third most common cause of chronic disability in older adults, and about one fall in ten ends in a fracture.
  • Metabolism. Muscle is one of the main places the body handles sugar. Less muscle tends to go with worse glucose handling, and with gaining fat more easily.
  • Independence. Standing up from a chair, carrying the shopping, walking up stairs. All of that sits on strength. When it goes, a piece of independence goes with it.

A 2025 review links sarcopenia with higher mortality, a lower quality of life, and physical dependence. That is why it pays to act earlier, not once the problem is obvious.

Lever 1: strength training (this is where it turns)

If I could give you one single thing, it is this. Strength training is the foundation of dealing with sarcopenia. Not a stroll, not a bike, not yoga, even though all of those are fine. You have to load the muscle against resistance, or it has no reason to stay.

A 2024 review puts it clearly: strength training can strongly switch on muscle protein building and slow muscle loss in older people. It also helps after illness or a hospital stay, when muscle disappears especially fast.

In practice, from the recommendations in the review on sarcopenia and falls:

  • Train the main muscle groups 2 to 3 times a week.
  • Do 2 to 3 sets per muscle group, around 5 to 8 reps with a heavier load, or a lighter load (50 to 80% of your maximum) taken to fatigue.
  • Add a little over time. Muscle grows only when you keep asking it for more.

You do not have to go to a gym. A controlled 2024 study showed that even a seated exercise program for older adults in care homes improved muscle mass, strength, and function after 12 weeks. Body weight, bands, or water bottles are enough. What matters is regularity, and actually loading the muscle. If you are starting after a long break, or you have trouble with joints or your back, have a trainer or a physical therapist show you the first sessions.

Lever 2: enough protein (and why you need more after 50)

Here is the catch a lot of people do not know about. With aging comes what is called anabolic resistance. Simply put, an older muscle responds less well to protein. The same portion that, at 30, switched muscle building on fully, only gives it a partial nudge after 60.

A 2024 review describes this anabolic resistance as one of the main reasons muscle disappears with age. The fix is logical: a higher protein target, so the muscle still gets built despite the weaker response.

  • Many expert recommendations for older adults aim at about 1.2 to 1.5 g of protein per kilogram of body weight a day (about 0.55 to 0.7 g per pound), which is more than the classic minimum for young adults.
  • Spread it through the day. A solid dose of protein at each main meal works better than throwing it all into dinner.
  • Quality matters. Reviews mention the amino acid leucine (there is a lot of it in whey, eggs, meat, and dairy) as a strong trigger for building muscle.

A small note, to be safe: if you have kidney disease, or a doctor has limited your protein for any reason, talk about raising it with them first, not from an article on the internet. How much protein to eat in general is in this article.

Treat it as fuel. Training gives the muscle the signal to grow. Protein supplies the bricks it is built from. One without the other limps.

The strongest version is both together

Now the most important part. Training and protein are not two competing options you pick between. They work together, and they fill each other's gaps.

The biggest review on this, from 2025, shows it: a network meta-analysis of 96 studies and more than 7,500 participants. The most effective strategy against sarcopenia was strength training (and balance training) combined with protein nutrition. It improved grip strength, walking speed, and muscle mass more than training alone or nutrition alone. The improvements in walking and in physical function were also clinically meaningful, meaning you would notice them in ordinary life.

A practical plan you can start this week:

  • Do 2 to 3 strength sessions a week, at home with a band or your body weight if you want.
  • Add a proper portion of protein to every main meal, and keep an eye on the daily total.
  • Be patient, and stay with it. Muscle is not built in a week. It is built over months. And then it holds.

You are not 50, and you are reading this for a parent? Even better. The earlier a base of muscle is built, the more you have to draw on after 50. Creatine can be a small extra on top of lifting for some people, which is covered in creatine for women. It does not replace the two levers above.
