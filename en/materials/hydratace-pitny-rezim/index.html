Hydration

Water is the most underrated "supplement" for performance, focus and appetite. Lose a few percent of body water and strength, endurance and mood all drop. And you will often mistake thirst for hunger. Here is how much to drink in real life, and how to actually do it. Practical, and based on the research.

How much to drink

Water is an essential nutrient. Digestion, blood flow and body temperature all sit on it [2]. A workable starting point is about 30-35 ml per kg of body weight per day: at 80 kg that is 80 × 30-35 ml = about 2.4-2.8 L from all sources. You take in part of it (about 20-30%) from food. You drink the rest. Reference values put total daily intake from all fluids and food at about 2.0 L for women and 2.5 L for men [3].

That is a starting point, not a ceiling. Add more for the situation:

  • Training and sweat. Under load, in the heat, and in people who sweat a lot, losses go up. Replace fluid based on how much you sweat, not on one fixed number [1].
  • Heat and humidity. On a hot day, or in an overheated room, you evaporate more without noticing. Add some.
  • Body size. A bigger, more muscular body needs more than a small one. That is why counting per kilogram makes sense.
  • Urine colour as a check. Pale straw = fine. Dark = drink. It is the simplest feedback you have.
Main point: Start at about 30-35 ml/kg and adjust for activity, heat and sweat [1,2]. Drink across the day, not only when your mouth is dry. Thirst arrives late.

Electrolytes: it is not only water

Water on its own is not the whole story. Electrolytes matter too, mainly sodium and potassium. Sweat costs you sodium first. Sodium keeps fluid in the body and helps you absorb it. That is why a drink with a little sodium stays in you longer than plain water [8]. Potassium works inside cells, and together with sodium it runs nerve and muscle signalling and water balance.

  • Sodium you normally get from food (salt). On a long session, with heavy sweating, or in the heat, it is worth replacing it on purpose.
  • Potassium is in fruit, vegetables, potatoes and legumes. A varied diet usually covers it.
  • On efforts longer than about 60-90 min, or when you sweat hard, use a drink with sodium, not water alone [1,8].
  • A normal day, an office, an easy session? Water and ordinary food are plenty. You do not need an expensive sports drink.

Signs of dehydration

Dehydration does not start with thirst. Thirst is a late signal. The body flags low fluid earlier, and it is easy to ignore:

  • Dark urine, and not much of it. The most reliable and fastest hydration check [4].
  • Fatigue, headache, a worse mood. Even mild dehydration (a loss of about 1-2% of body weight as water) worsens mood and raises perceived effort [5,6].
  • A dry mouth, sticky saliva, thirst. Drink before it gets here.
  • Worse focus and a "foggy" head. The brain reacts sensitively to a drop in fluid [6].
Rule of thumb: A loss of about 2% of body weight from fluid already cuts performance and thinking in a measurable way [4,6]. At 80 kg that is only about 1.6 kg. A hard session can take that off you easily.

Effect on performance and the brain

This is why it is not a cosmetic detail. Dehydration is one of the cheapest ways to undercut your own performance:

  • Physical performance. Fluid loss above about 2% of body weight reduces endurance and strength, and raises heart strain and perceived effort, especially in the heat [1,4].
  • Brain and mood. Mild dehydration worsens attention and memory and raises fatigue and irritability. Shown in women [5] and in men [6].
  • Cooling. Less water = worse cooling through sweat = you overheat sooner and performance drops [1].

In other words: before you fine-tune the last percent of your diet or your training, make sure you are drinking enough. It is the fastest win.

Thirst vs. hunger: don't mix them up

The thirst and hunger centres in the brain sit close together, so a thirst signal is easy to read as an urge to eat. The result: you eat when you were actually just thirsty. Water can also help energy intake directly. A higher water intake is linked with lower energy intake and better weight control [7].

  • A craving "for something" between meals? Drink first and wait about 10 minutes. Often it was thirst [7].
  • A glass of water before a meal fills you a little more and helps you not overeat [7].
  • Water has no calories and takes up space in the stomach. A cheap helper in a deficit.

Caffeine and alcohol vs. hydration

Coffee and beer collect a lot of myths. Let's straighten them:

1Coffee does not "dry you out"
Ordinary doses of caffeine have only a mild diuretic effect, and they count toward daily fluid like anything else. Coffee and tea hydrate about as well as water [8].
2Alcohol dehydrates
It suppresses the hormone that holds water, so you urinate more and hydration gets worse. Beer is also not among the better hydrating drinks [8].
3Water in between
With alcohol, put a glass of water between drinks. You cut the losses and the morning after.
4Watch the whole day
Morning coffee is fine. If the day is only coffee and beer, add plain water on top.

Practical tips: how to drink more

Knowing how much to drink is half of it. The other half is actually drinking it. These habits make it automatic:

1A bottle in sight
What you see, you drink. Keep a bottle on the desk, in the car, in the bag. Out of sight = forgotten.
2A glass in the morning
Right after waking, drink a glass of water. You went hours overnight without drinking, and you start the day down.
3Attach it to habits
Drink with every meal, with coffee, and on the way to the bathroom. Hook drinking onto something you already do.
4Around training
Drink before, during and after the session, and add sodium when you sweat a lot [1,8]. Use urine colour as the check [4].
Action step: This week do just one thing: work out about 30-35 ml/kg as your base target, get a bottle, and keep it in sight. In the heat and around training, add more and add sodium [1,8]. Once that runs by itself, worry about the details. Be Effective!
